# Stop decorating your game and start playing it 

## Prelude 

Few days ago, I went to Arcane Alley, Jakarta's (Indonesia's capital) biggest Ren Faire. It is their second time hosting, and things were really great. It's too eurocentric at times, but hey, that's essentially what Rein Faires are. The event also accommodates an art market as well, more oriented to accessories or fantasy stuff. 

In AA, I visited the LFG booth where they were selling some of the modules that the LFG community made (including mines!!!). Being a broke college kid with my family's financial situation rocky, I saved up for 2 weeks and can only buy Isaac's Bastion Elysium (it's super duper good, y'all gotta check it out). After buying, I immediately wanted to run it there (I actually did), but I had a thought in my mind. I paid around 180k, which is around 10 dollars, which I guess is the standard price for TTRPG zines (I'm going to call adventure, modules, etc as zines for clarity from now on) internationally, but an Indonesian and for me personally, this was a hefty purchase. 

I kept thinking, with 180k, what can I buy...?

My mind got transported to Comifuro (Comic Frontier), Indonesia's biggest art market. Think of Anime Expo in the US. CF 22, the previous one, had around 20,000 visitors with 1,500 circles selling. It's really, really big. Back to topic, with 180K (although not much for CF ngl), you could buy a lot of...stickers with it! Say sticker sheet cost 50k, you could buy around 3 of them, and then maybe an art print. Point is, which that amount, I can buy so many other fanmerch. But I thought to myself and I don't think I regret buying Isaac's zine at all. I've played it before, it was fun, and I want to support the local scene. But is it really the case here? 

You gotta understand Indonesia's post pandemic cultural climate. I'm sure that other countries also follow suit, but after pandemic, "Nerd" hobbies has exponentially grown. Anime, which used to be cringe and looked down upon, became a very mainstream media right now, essentially pop culture. "Wibu", the Indonesian term for weaboos/weebs, changed from a mocking expression into a proud identity. 

The expansion of anime's fame also results in the boom of fandoms as well, impacting young people. As a result, you would see a lot of younger generation expresses their interests a lot via their pins in their bags, or wearing their ita bags with their oshis (favourite person/character) around. Cosplay is now mainstream, with anime costumes that can be rental-ed at a cheap price. You can just search these cosplay rentals at mere instagram account, which can get shipped to you within mere days and be used on cosplay events temporarily. Your identity now revolve whether or not if you are in a certain fandom or not, this is the new social currency.

Back to Comifuro, which started as just a simple art market hosted by Universitas Indonesia (Indonesia University) has expanded very rapidly onto this hyper-consumeristic, commercialized malls. Attendees are hypnotized, conditioned to drop hundreds of thousands of Rupiah on mass-produced, corporate IP fanmerch (acrylic standees, prints, keychains) without a second thought. Of course there's some original stuff sold but compared to merch with a fandom attached to it? It pales in comparison. 

So you understand when I bought the zine, I felt a bit of shock, but after thinking, it's different. I'm not buying a fanmerch. I'm not buying simply a decoration. No, I'm purchasing an engine designed to generate shared experiences and memories.

## Fast Merch vs Slow Zines 

The harsh reality of indie fanmerch artists is that our country's economy is so fucked the best way to gain profit or to minimize losses is to invest in cheap materials for your fanmerch. Artists don't have that much capital to spend on quality merch, and the customers don't have the buying power to buy on a high quality manufacturer. As a result? Acrylics scratch, stickers peel and fade, enamel pins slowly tarnish. Even if you take care of it nicely, it's not your fault. It's the materials. It's the same mindset as fast fashion, where if you want to sell the most fanmerch, you would need to follow the most trending fandoms and latch upon it. Hoyoverse merch? You got it. And to keep up with that trend, you mass produce the merch within the fastest production possible. It's...it's bleak honestly, but the reality is that this is what everyone wants. Some people are aware of this reality but they are unfortunately are attached to this ecosystem. Unless you build a following or create a brand for yourself, it's really hard to get profit. 

Compare this with a zine. Although not likely, but if I stained my zines with coffee, or accidentally folded it in an permanent way, the rules and narrative inside still persist. I can always use it again and again (albeit with shitty quality but still works!) Your zine will always have the magic necessary for you to run the game. [Bare](https://barenuckelavee.bearblog.dev/no-one-can-take-your-game/) tells us that there is genuinely a very accesible way of playing ttrpgs. Even with torn paper and a paperclip spinner, you're still able to experience TTRPGs Even when you're at your lowest point in your life, spending time in a place where people outside has already lost hope for you, TTRPG will always be there for us. The real game exist in our heads as a shared imagination, every physical object down there is just a vehicle, a mere tool. Once you learn a game, it will forever live in you. It's immortal.

## Active Experience Loop 

It's not Rose's Blog if I don't include a bullshit theoryslop. But I'll make it simple don't worry.

There's a fundamental reason when we approach these fanmerch and TTRPG zines. When we examine fanmerch, it operates linearly. People buy (and keep) fanmerch because they evoke a feeling towards them. Mostly love, or adoration, even obsession. But the point, is that it's linear (Object ➔ Feeling). You look at it, you feel a brief spark of aesthetic joy or self expression, and the interaction abruptly ends. This is more prevalent when you realize your attachment to said fanmerch depends if you are currently invested or not, because there will be times where you don't feel as passionate to your fandoms, which will impact your feelings towards your favourite fanmerch. Perhaps you used to like Reigen Arataka from Mob Pyscho and built a shrine, but years later your taste has changed and you no longer feel that spark towards him. The feeling's gone. To you, that Reigen church isn't anything special or worthy. The value stops becoming a self expression and instead is just a fleeting visual decoration on your desk.

How about zines you say? Well, instead of a passive loop, tis a active loop! How you say? Because what makes TTRPG zines different is that they provide you with a unique experience every time, and you get your feelings sprung from your experience! (Object ➔ Experience ➔ Feeling). This doesn't mean you can't derive immediate feelings from a zine. You can definitely read an awesome Appendix N entry and feel motivated, but the main purpose of zines is that you would *need* participation! You can't just passively consume it like a sticker! You gotta read it carefully, internalize the designer's mechanics, and actively translate it to the table with your friends. Not to mention that every table runs the zine differently, resulting in a very emergent experiences that no table will ever have the same one. 

But this absolute requirement for mental effort is exactly why the modern consumerist mindset struggles to buy zines. Over on the Roll to Doubt blog, Weird Writer captures this perfectly in their essay [An Illiterate Hobby](https://rolltodoubt.wordpress.com/2026/06/08/an-illiterate-hobby/). They point out a frustrating phenomenon: many modern hobbyists actively hate reading. They demand bullet points, aesthetic layouts, and hyper-simplified text because they want to skip the effort of actually digesting a designer's words and jump straight into the instant gratification of playing.

This isn't just a TTRPG problem though, it is a direct reflection of our current attention economy. Fanmerch is so easy to buy because it caters perfectly to a culture addicted to short-form content. A keychain or a standee requires zero literacy and zero attention span, it just immediately hands you the emotion you want. Zines demand the exact opposite. Yes, reading takes effort, it takes time which not all of us have in abundance, but I found the struggle in itself to be also the key to happiness.

## The D in Indonesia stands for Dice Goblin 

Let's step back for a bit. I mentioned that Indonesia had this massive cultural change in pop culture, but does this effect TTRPGs as well? Kinda. 

Isaac explains this [well](https://afraidofencounters.bearblog.dev/afraid-of-communities-indonesian-tabletop-rpg-scene-lfg/), but prior to pandemic, TTRPGs aren't that massive in the local scene. Afterwards? It got bigger. That, and Raditya Dika made DnD more popular than ever. There is a *lot* of people entering this hobby. The difference is how are exactly being introduced. 

There is nothing wrong with being introduced from youtube videos, like Raditya Dika or Critical Role's actual plays, but you cannot deny that being introduced via a visual medium primarily influences their perception of the game. I'm talking about the **aesthetic** of the game.

Think about what actually happens in an Actual Play video. Camera cuts to the player, dramatic music swells, they shake a handful of gorgeous, glittering resin, and the whole table screams when it lands on a 20. You see this a lot in both long form content and short form tiktok videos, they get the most likes, views, and engagement. The dice become the physical manifestation of the hype. TTRPGs happen almost entirely in our heads, making the dice the only physical anchor to the real world. It is incredibly easy to spend money on a beautiful, tangible object you can hold, toss, and show off to your friends on Instagram.

On the flip side, reading a module feels like homework. As we established earlier, reading takes mental effort. A 40 page zine doesn't give you that immediate, tactile dopamine hit that a heavy metal D20 does. So, new players naturally gravitate toward the shiny things that make them feel exactly like the cool people on screen. The focus gets spent on the accessories moreso than the books themselves. Adding to the fact that as newcomers, you will feel that GM-ing will be a chore, so any kind of prep or learning (in this case, trying to read modules) will be less incentivized. The only learning that you'll feel motivated is probably either writing more backstory for your character or optimizing your build. I mean, why do that when your GM handles that right? You can just sit back, rp your PC, and roll the pretty dice.

So people *can* afford to buy 250k resin dice sets from out of the country. I've seen people wearing  actual mini cases for their dices only. Oh my. People can buy dices but not...modules? 

This is what happens when a hobby gets filtered through the glossy lens of YouTube. As [Unturned Hovel](https://unturnedhovel.bearblog.dev/the-product-boomerang/) points out in their critique of modern tabletop consumerism, the scene is constantly being pushed toward fancy greebles, bespoke components, and aesthetic fidget toys designed to look great on camera. It turns the game into a mere aesthetic checklist. Even if the TTRPG culture in the west and in the local scene are different, there's still a one universal trend: Consumerism.

And as [elmc.at](https://elmc.at/death-of-a-hobby/) observes in _Death of a Hobby_, this creates an exhausting cycle where hobbyists collect gorgeous, unplayed physical objects just to stare at them on a shelf, completely neglecting the actual campaign. This extends to dice hoarding, buying endless sets of dice merely _decorates_ the game. It gives you a quick, effortless hit of gamer identity without requiring you to do any actual prep, reading, or heavy lifting. Meanwhile, buying a local zine actually _sustains_ the ecosystem. If everyone keeps funneling their money into plastic math rocks while local designers starve, we'll end up with a scene full of incredible dice collections and absolutely zero games to play.

## Legitimacy and the "Hobby Business"

Let's talk about the elephant in the room: capitalism guilt. I get it. When you see a friend charging 180k for a passion project, a tiny part of your brain whispers that maybe they're selling out, that they're becoming the very corporate shill we complain about. But charging money for your art isn't inherently evil.

Over at [Prismatic Wasteland](https://www.prismaticwasteland.com/blog/hey-you-got-commercialization-in-my-hobby-also-zinequest-4), they bring up this fascinating economic concept called a _Veblen good_. Basically, putting a price tag on a game gives it psychological legitimacy. Think about it. If Isaac handed out _Bastion Elysium_ for free, half the people who took it would toss it in a drawer and treat it like a disposable flyer. It's like the flyers ads you get when you open your house's door, probably about a developer advertising about a newly built complex. Paying 180k IDR kinda forces you to take the designer's mechanics seriously. You bought it, so you're going to read it and run it. The price tag ensures the art actually gets used at the table. Same thing when you buy a video game, you feel compelled to finish it even at times when you don't feel a lot because, well, you *did* pay for it. 

Plus, we have to recognize the reality of making these games. [Matthew Colville](https://www.youtube.com/watch?app=desktop&v=jVIWSuMgVT8&ra=m) breaks this down perfectly in his video about the "DIY Hobby Business." Indie creators really aren't megacorporations hoarding wealth on yachts. They are just regular people charging money to justify the immense amount of time, sweat, and tears they pour into a project. The whole scene is still entirely driven by that scrappy, punk DIY ethos. Most of the times, the money made from selling your zines won't be enough to actually pay rent and that's okay! Think of it as extra pocket money.

And here is the absolute best part: that 180k IDR doesn't vanish into a billionaire's offshore bank account. When I buy my friend's zine, I know exactly where that cash goes. They will probably use that exact same money to back another Indonesian creator's project on itch.io, commission a local illustrator for their next book, or even buy fanmerch at the next convention. The money stays right here. It feeds a sustainable, circular ecosystem within our own indie community instead of bleeding out to a faceless corporation. 

## The True Emotional ROI

Let's put that 180k IDR into perspective. People in Jakarta will routinely drop 180k on two premium iced coffees in Senopati, or a quick aesthetic dinner that lasts exactly 45 minutes and is completely forgotten by Tuesday. We spend that money constantly without batting an eye.

Spending that exact same amount on a TTRPG zine gives you a fundamentally different return. You're buying hours of saved GM prep. You're buying a structured excuse to get three or four of your closest friends around a table for an entire evening of active, unpredictable chaos. The emotional return on investment of a tabletop session vastly outweighs the brief dopamine hit of displaying a new acrylic keychain on your bag or adding a seventh set of resin dice to your carrying case.

So here is my challenge to you. The next time you find yourself at Comifuro, or scrolling through Shopee looking for another decorative dice tray you absolutely do not need, stop. Take that damn money and invest it in a shared experience. Go find a local creator's booth at a zine fest, or browse the Indonesian tabletop scene on itch.io. Buy their game, read it, and actually play it with your friends.

Our scene needs players, not just decorators.
