# Gambit Stance is Even Objectively Correct*

## preamble

The player advice blogwagon wrapped up recently, and during that whole run there was a lot of awesome advice. One of the more "popular" posts was Sera's [Pawn Stance is Objectively Correct](https://diceexploder.com/posts/pawn-stance-is-objectively-correct) on Dice Exploder! I personally love when a title is bold (and kinda clickbaity (I think I write like this too)). At the time there were a few things I disagreed with in how the main idea was presented, though overall I liked it and ended up applying it in my games here and there afterwards. If you haven't read it yet, you gotta go read it first. This blog essentially builds off it (and innately glazes it). Still, if I can summarize it rq, it boils down to two points:

- Your character is a toy, so treat them like one.
- Your fun matters more than canon or logic.

By all means, it's a good post!

Then a few days ago, someone showed me a couple of videos made in response to the blog, and this greatly displeases me 👎👎👎👎👎👎 immense ragebait. Which is a shame because it's such a good blog, but the responses are so visceral? So this post is my excuse to respond to the points in those videos.

With that in mind, the actual heart of this blog was already in my head wayyyyy before those videos dropped. With this "gambit" stance, I'm bringing a more focused stance built around the roleplaying aspect.

Before we start, go read [Billhook's blog on player advice](https://billhook.bearblog.dev/advice-on-play/) (very awesome advice too), specifically rule #1, which emphasizes that everyone's fun at the table is your responsibility.

So we've got Sera's post and Billhook's blog... let's combine them...

If your character is a toy you can break for your own fun... you're also allowed to break them for your friends' fun.

## let's address the elephant in the room first

So how did the blog break containment?? Well, Ben Milton (Questing Beast) made [a video backing Sera's Pawn Stance post](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=thaQrqpMsUU), framing it around "tactical infinity" and solving problems in the dungeon. That kicked off a wave of angry response videos treating Pawn Stance like some anti roleplay board game heresy.

First up is Matt from [The Tomb of Lime](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xu0dD2920i8). He argues that the unique magic of RPGs ("the thing") is "being able to inhabit a character and make meaningful decisions as that character in a fictional world." When he gets to Sera's examples of the cowardly character or the broken glasses, he basically says "my brother in Christ, you chose to play these characters" and tells people to just "make better characters." In his eyes, using Pawn Stance when things get annoying means you "don't really like roleplay. They like beer and pretzel fun." He even compares playing RPGs for "the pleasures of human interaction" to buying a hammer and complaining that it's bad at dislodging kale from between your teeth.

Which... sure? Inhabiting a character purely for solo agency sounds awesome if you're playing Fallout New Vegas alone in your bedroom at 2 AM or playing your own solo rpg/journaling game. At an actual table though, treating "the pleasures of human interaction" like some side quest you're doing wrong with a hammer is insane bro. Obsessing over your own solo character agency can easily turn into four guys silently parallel playing in the same room. At some point it's just 4 people who coincidentally roleplay at the same time rather than 4 people roleplaying together and interacting with each other.

(Update: Matt actually dropped a [follow up video](https://www.google.com/search?q=INSERT_LINK_HERE) while I was writing this, where he defines "Capital R Roleplay" as a loop between your character and the GM's world verisimilitude... literally comparing it to reading a good book you can explore. He even says stepping out of character means you've stopped playing the game. Again, zero mention of the other players sitting at the table with you... #noticing)

Then there's [the "apple pie" response video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kylpddQDYzQ). The creator goes right after Ben's video and argues that making decisions strictly from your character's perspective and limited knowledge is the literal "apple" in the RPG pie. If you don't like the apples, he says you should escape the "D&D ditch" and go play a board game or a wargame (calling it hanging a picture with your cell phone). He also waves away conflicts between what the player wants and what the character wants as "so bad faith and low trust" that he won't even entertain them, and lumps "things like metagaming and cheating" together because acting on outside knowledge violates what you're at the table to do.

![son](https://i.ibb.co.com/YTf9Cpk8/b181860d02096d8c06fd949bb76bd79c.jpg)

Like... brother. What tables are you playing at?? Two things. First of all, a character's personality doesn't mean that they'll act very black and white. A character who's chatty and loud and outgoing will still go nonverbal or silent once in a while. A shy, introverted bookworm can speak their heart out given the correct situation. Personality is defined as "the quality or state of being a person", and guess what? A character's state can always change any time. They aren't rigid math equations.

Second, people bend their character's strict perspective _because_ they love roleplaying and want more of it with their friends. When you actually love RP, you're constantly hunting for tiny excuses to say yes to a scene... stretching your character's stubbornness just enough to ask a nosy question, or softening their aloofness so a conversation actually gets to happen instead of dying on the vine. Bending your sheet to hype up a friend's monologue or jump into a messy argument is literally how party dynamics evolve and how you stumble into the best moments of the whole campaign. Refusing to ever budge from what's written on paper just turns your guy into an NPC stuck on a dialogue loop. Telling someone who bends their character to chase more scenes like that to "escape the D&D ditch" makes zero sense. (Also, yeah, sure, toxic players hiding behind "it's what my character would do" are technically using Pawn Stance to be a pest. Assholes are gonna be selfish in any stance anyways.)

Honestly, the funniest part is that even Ben Milton falls into the exact same trap as the guys mad at him. Look at all three videos side by side. Questing Beast says use Pawn Stance so _I_ can solve dungeons. Tomb of Lime says inhabit _my_ character so _I_ feel meaningful agency. Apple Pie guy says stay in _my_ character's head or else _I_ should play a different game. Every single one of them frames tabletop RPGs as **Me vs. The Game World**. Somehow nobody in this entire YouTube discourse stopped to remember that there are other human beings sitting right next to you...

## the sad version of "it's what my character would do"

Let's push back on both "just make better characters" and "bad faith and low trust" for a second. Everyone already clowns on the chaotic rogue who steals from the party and lies about character accuracy. We've beaten that horse into glue.

What nobody talks about is the quiet, depressing version of the exact same problem... and it happens at super chill, high trust tables with _good_ characters all the time. You've probably done it yourself. You make a brooding mercenary or a shy scholar, and then you spend 90 minutes sitting in the corner of the tavern saying nothing because "well, they wouldn't trust these strangers yet." Meanwhile, the actual human behind the character sheet is literally starving to join the banter. Nothing about that is bad faith trolling, and you didn't accidentally build a terrible character who hates water in a pirate campaign. You're just letting a piece of paper bully you into sitting alone.

It starves other people too. Say your friend has been sitting on a cool backstory monologue for three sessions, practically begging for someone to ask about their cursed sword. Your grumpy fighter _realistically_ wouldn't care enough to ask. So you stay 100% faithful to your sheet, keep your mouth shut, and everyone stares at the battlemap in awkward silence. Congrats on preserving your immersion, I guess...

## enter: gambit stance

Okay, enough complaining. Let's steal Sera's chess gimmick and push it further.

In chess, a gambit is when you intentionally sacrifice one of your own pieces to blow the board wide open for a sick play. You take a small hit right now so the whole game gets way more exciting.

That's **Gambit Stance**. You deliberately throw away your character's pristine consistency or dignity for thirty seconds so another player gets to shine.

Actually, [Rus's post on Competitive Storytelling](https://rusroleplayrants.com/2026/11/22/competitive-storytelling/) nails why doing this feels so weird at first. Rus points out that table time and spotlight are basically a zero sum game... if you want to step aside or let your character look dumb for the broader story, you have to "swallow the 'loss'" at personal cost.

And honestly? Yeah, on paper it _does_ look like a loss (which just proves calling it a gambit is even more accurate lol). Here's the thing though... if you're actually emotionally invested in your friends' characters, watching them pop off _is_ a win for you too. Sure, maybe the game rules themselves aren't magically making it "collaborative storytelling" on their own. Whatever. When you willingly sacrifice your own cool moment or your character's strict consistency just because it makes the table happier, that's collaborative storytelling in the way that actually counts. Why wouldn't you take that trade??

Quick shoutout to [To Be Resolved's post on making characters you'll actually enjoy playing](https://tbr.bearblog.dev/making-a-character-youll-enjoy-playing/). Building pre game dyads and intertwined backstories during session zero is genuinely goated advice, and you should totally do that. Still, even the best designed party is gonna hit session seven where your character's internal logic tells you to walk away from the fun. When that happens, you pull the gambit.

## how to actually do this (without feeling like a fraud)

If you've been conditioned to treat character accuracy like a sacred oath, breaking it feels weird at first. Here's how you actually pull it off at the table.

**Reclaim social metagaming.** Remember how the Apple Pie video lumped "metagaming and cheating" together because acting on outside knowledge ruins the table? Forget that. When people treat metagaming like a slur, they're usually thinking of guys secretly googling monster stat blocks under the table. Social metagaming is a straight up superpower. If you notice a quiet player hasn't spoken in twenty minutes, use your "outside knowledge" as a human being with eyeballs, walk your stoic warrior right over, and ask them a weirdly nosy question to hand them the mic. Does your character _really_ care about their dead mentor? Who gaf!! Your friend wants to do a scene, so be the doorway.

(Also, play more story games please. PbtA games especially... a bunch of them literally bake social metagaming right into the rules with stuff like Strings/Bonds, so the system actively rewards you for meddling in your friends' arcs. Once you play a few of those, tossing someone the mic in a trad game feels way more natural.)

**Be the enabler.** Sometimes your friend pitches a hilarious, terrible plan that your high intelligence wizard would realistically shoot down in two seconds. Break character just enough to say "screw it, I'm in." Even better, play the exasperated straight man who complains the whole time while _still_ tagging along. Keep the bit alive instead of killing the momentum with logic.

**Let your character be a prop.** Sera already said your character is a toy for your enjoyment. Push it one step further... your character is also a toy for your friends' enjoyment. Ask a dumb question so the lore nerd gets to info dump. Step straight onto an obvious pressure plate so the healer gets to feel like a hero. Your character sheet can take the hit.

## the actual apple in the pie

I'm gonna steal that Apple Pie metaphor right back because it's actually kind of useful once you fix it. Collaborative play with the people sitting around you is the actual apple in a tabletop RPG pie. Strict character perspective is just the cinnamon... it tastes awesome when it works, and you're totally allowed to skip it when it's ruining the slice. Nobody is gonna look back on your campaign a year from now and complain that your edgy rogue was 5% too friendly during session eight. They _will_ remember the insane, hilarious scene your friend got to pull off because you passed them the ball.

Deep down, you're at the table to play pretend with your friends. Don't sit in the corner alone just to prove you're good at acting.
